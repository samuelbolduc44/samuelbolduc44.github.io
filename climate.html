The El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO)

What is ENSO?

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is a complex and significant climate phenomenon that influences weather patterns globally. Characterized by variations in sea surface temperatures (SSTs) and atmospheric pressure across the equatorial Pacific Ocean, ENSO manifests itself primarily in two phases: El Niño, with systematically warmer-than-average SSTs, and La Niña, with cooler-than-average SSTs in the eastern Pacific Ocean. These events have a profound impact on global climate, altering weather patterns, ocean conditions, and atmospheric pressure systems. Such changes can have widespread consequences for agriculture, water resources, and socioeconomic systems worldwide.

The delayed-action oscillator (DAO)

The earliest explanations of ENSO date back to Bjerknes, who identified the Bjerknes positive feedback: a warm SST anomaly in the eastern Pacific weakens the zonal SST gradient, reduces the Walker circulation and easterly winds, and thereby promotes further eastward spreading of warm water. However, Bjerknes did not identify a mechanism capable of reversing the warming phase and initiating a transition to the opposite (La~Niña) phase. It became clear that, in addition to the Bjerknes positive feedback, a negative feedback mechanism is required to reverse the coupled ocean--atmosphere system from a warm (cold) to a cold (warm) phase.

Several conceptual frameworks formalize this interplay between positive and negative feedbacks. Conceptual frameworks like these condense the essential physics into manageable mathematical forms, facilitating analytical study and intuition building. This contrasts with large complex systems that model the oceans and atmosphere in considerable detail, and yet are much less amenable to mathematical discussion.

For my research, I focus on the DAO paradigm introduced by Suarez and Schopf in 1988. Under normal conditions in the equatorial Pacific Ocean, easterly trade winds drive warm surface water toward the western equatorial Pacific, inducing upwelling in the east and establishing the Walker circulation. This zonal pattern produces warm, wet conditions in the west and cooler, drier conditions in the east. A perturbation that weakens the trade winds allows warm water to shift eastward. This perturbation generates propagating oceanic waves that travel along the thermocline -- the transition zone between the warmer surface waters and the cooler deep waters. Westward-propagating Rossby waves reflect at the western boundary and return as Kelvin waves, providing a negative delayed feedback that 'terminates' the event.

Current Working Project

Comprehensive bifurcation analysis of the pfSS model

We perform a comprehensive bifurcation analysis of the proposed pfSS model, with the nonlinearity strength treated as the primary continuation parameter to systematically map the system's dynamical landscape. This project is done with the continuation package DDEBIFTOOL in MATLAB. We demonstrate that chaos emerges via overlapping resonance tongues, bringing the model's behavior closer to the variability observed in ENSO. This perspective is consistent with previous studies in which rich dynamics were found in the low-frequency regime.

Generalization of Feedback with implicit state-dependent delay in a conceptual model of El Niño

Generalization to arbitrarly number of nestings and for arbtrary bounded and measurable forcing functions.

In state-dependent delayed negative feedback systems, the delay is often not a prescribed function of the current state but rather the solution of an implicit algebraic equation[1]. Indeed, consider a feedback signal that departs at time $t$ and returns after a lag $\sigma$, arriving at time $s = t + \sigma$. If the travel time is a reference lag $\bar\tau$ corrected by a state-dependent response at the moment of departure, then $\sigma = \bar\tau+\lambda(h(t))$. But since $t = s - \sigma$, the departure state satisfies $h(t) = h(s-\sigma)$, and the lag equation becomes $\sigma = \bar\tau+\lambda(h(s-\sigma))$. Renaming $s$ by $t$, the delay $\sigma(t)$ satisfies $\sigma(t) = \bar\tau+\lambda(h(t-\sigma(t)))$, and the system takes the form

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ddeimplicit} \left. \begin{aligned} h'(t) &= f(h(t-\sigma(t))), \\ 0 &= \bar\tau+\lambda(h(t-\sigma(t))) - \sigma(t). \end{aligned} \right\} \end{equation}

The delay $\sigma(t)$ determines which past moment $t-\sigma(t)$ is relevant, but the state at that moment in turn determines $\sigma(t)$: neither can be found independently of the other, and both must be determined simultaneously as a self-consistent pair. Our main result focuses on the regime where the implicit fixed-point problem is contractive. In this regime, delayed state dependence for a particular class of piecewise-smooth delay differential equations is dynamically invisible: introducing the implicit delay does not change the solution of the constant-delay problem.

[1] Walther, H.-O. (2014). Topics in delay differential equations. Jahresbericht der Deutschen Mathematiker-Vereinigung, 116(2), 87–114.

Generalization of Seasonal forcing dominated dynamics of a piecewise smooth Ghil-Zaliapin-Thompson ENSO model

Stability of a periodic orbit can be assessed by studying how small perturbations evolve under the dynamics. For a symmetric $n$:$s$-periodic orbit, this reduces to tracking the evolution of the orbit’s nondegenerate zeros. By analyzing how the zeros of a perturbed orbit shift over one period, we obtain a linear recurrence relation.
